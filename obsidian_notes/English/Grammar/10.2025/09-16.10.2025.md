# 09.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">Must and Have (got) to</span>

### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
Both **must** and **have (got) to** express **obligation or necessity**, but they differ slightly in use:
- **Must** — expresses **personal obligation** or **strong necessity** coming from the speaker.
    > “I must finish this project tonight.” (I feel it’s necessary.)
    
- **Have to / Have got to** — expresses **external obligation**, often from rules, situations, or others.
    > “I have to wear a uniform at work.” (It’s required by someone else.)

**Must** is mainly used in **present and future**, while **have to** is used in **all tenses**.
> “You had to call her yesterday.” ✅  
> “You musted call her yesterday.” ❌
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
|Tense|Positive|Negative|Question|
|---|---|---|---|
|Present|I **must** go. / I **have to** go.|I **mustn’t** go. / I **don’t have to** go.|**Must** I go? / **Do** I have to go?|
|Past|—|—|Did you have to go?|
|Present Perfect|—|—|Have you had to go?|
|Future|I’ll have to go.|I won’t have to go.|Will you have to go?|

> 💡 “Have got to” is mostly **British and informal**:  
> “I’ve got to tell you something!”
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
Standing before the council, he realized he **had to** choose between **what he must do** and **what he wanted to do**—a choice that would define him forever.
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
You must be kidding me, we hadn't to go there, they decided to break up with their shitty relationships. 
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _I must to go now._ → ✅ _I must go now._
2. ❌ _I don’t must do it._ → ✅ _I don’t have to do it._
3. ❌ _You mustn’t go = You don’t have to go._ → ⚠️ Wrong!
    - _You mustn’t go_ = It’s forbidden.
    - _You don’t have to go_ = It’s not necessary.
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **Must-see** — “This movie is a must-see!”
2. **Must-have** — “A good keyboard is a must-have for programmers.”
3. **Have got to** — “You’ve got to be kidding me!”
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Need to** — weaker necessity (practical, not moral).
    > “You need to sleep more.”
    
- **Should / Ought to** — advice or recommendation.
    > “You should rest.”
    
- **Be supposed to** — duty or expectation.
    > “You’re supposed to be here by 9.”

# 10.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">May and Might</span>

### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
**“May”** and **“might”** are modal verbs used to express **possibility**, **permission**, **requests**, and **suggestions**.
- **May** is slightly more formal and is often used for polite requests or permissions.
- **Might** usually expresses a **weaker** or more **uncertain** possibility.

🧭 **In general:**
> “May” = real or likely possibility.  
> “Might” = more remote or hypothetical possibility.
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
| Structure                               | Example                        | Meaning                         |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| **Subject + may/might + base verb**     | She **may come** later.        | It’s possible.                  |
| **Subject + may/might not + base verb** | He **might not agree**.        | It’s possible he won’t.         |
| **May/might + have + past participle**  | They **might have forgotten**. | It’s possible (in the past).    |
| **May/might be + -ing**                 | She **may be working** now.    | Possible action in progress.    |
| **May I + base verb**                   | **May I** sit here?            | Asking for permission (formal). |
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
She **might have accepted** the offer if it **had been** made earlier, but now it’s too late.
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
I might be following here even after a break up, but she has decided not to proceed our conversation and cut them off forever.
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _“Maybe I will go.”_ → ✔️ _“I may go.”_  
    🔹 _“Maybe”_ is an adverb, not a modal verb.
    
2. ❌ _“He may to come.”_ → ✔️ _“He may come.”_  
    🔹 Modals are **never followed by “to”**.
    
3. ❌ _“I might can help you.”_ → ✔️ _“I might help you.”_  
    🔹 Never use two modals together.
    
4. ⚠️ Don’t confuse _“might”_ with _“maybe”_:
    - _He might come._ ✅ (modal verb)
    - _Maybe he’ll come._ ✅ (adverb)
    - _He maybe come._ ❌
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **May I help you?** → polite offer / request.
2. **It might seem strange, but...** → introducing a surprising idea.
3. **May God bless you.** → formal or religious wish.
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Can / Could** → possibility or ability (less formal).
    > It _could_ rain later. (= It might rain.)
    
- **Must / Can’t** → strong certainty or impossibility.
    > He _must be_ tired. / He _can’t be_ serious.
    
- **May as well / Might as well** → weak suggestion.
    > We _might as well_ go home — nothing’s happening here.
    
- **Maybe / Perhaps** → adverbs of possibility.
    > _Maybe_ he’ll call later.

# 11.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">Need(n’t), Don’t Need To, and Don’t Have To</span>

### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
These three expressions all refer to **lack of necessity or obligation**, but they are used slightly differently in formality, structure, and tone.

- **Needn’t** (modal) → means _“it is not necessary to”_ and is slightly more formal or written.
- **Don’t need to** (semi-modal) → means _“it isn’t necessary to”_, used in everyday speech.
- **Don’t have to** → also means _“it isn’t necessary to”_, but it’s the most neutral and common in spoken English.

🧭 **All mean there is no obligation**, but **mustn’t** ≠ **needn’t** →
> _Mustn’t = prohibition_ (“You mustn’t park here” = not allowed)  
> _Needn’t = lack of necessity_ (“You needn’t park here” = you don’t have to; it’s optional)
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
| Structure                               | Example                                   | Meaning                                       |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Needn’t + base verb**                 | You **needn’t worry**.                    | It’s not necessary.                           |
| **Don’t / doesn’t need to + base verb** | She **doesn’t need to come**.             | It’s not necessary.                           |
| **Didn’t need to + base verb**          | We **didn’t need to pay** for parking.    | It wasn’t necessary (and we didn’t).          |
| **Needn’t have + past participle**      | You **needn’t have cooked** so much food. | It wasn’t necessary, _but you did it anyway_. |
| **Don’t / doesn’t have to + base verb** | You **don’t have to call** him now.       | Optional action, no obligation.               |
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
 You **needn’t have brought** your laptop — the office computers are already configured for you.
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
I was trying to pull everything on my, take all actions, making every effort was only from me, so I needn't to think about it anymore, it's my freedom. 
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _You mustn’t be late if you don’t want to._  
     ✅ _You **needn’t** be late if you don’t want to._  
     🔹 “Mustn’t” = forbidden, not “not necessary.”
    
2. ❌ _You didn’t need have to come._  
     ✅ _You **needn’t have come**._  
     🔹 Only “needn’t have” expresses unnecessary past actions.
    
3. ⚠️ _Don’t need to_ vs _Needn’t_  
     - _You don’t need to call me._ ✅ (normal, neutral)  
     - _You needn’t call me._ ✅ (formal, polite, British tone)
    
4. ⚠️ _Didn’t need to_ ≠ _Needn’t have_  
     - _We didn’t need to buy tickets_ → we knew in advance it wasn’t necessary.  
     - _We needn’t have bought tickets_ → we bought them, but later realized it was unnecessary.
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **You needn’t worry.** → classic reassurance phrase.
2. **I don’t have to decide now.** → everyday speech for optional actions.
3. **We didn’t need to hurry.** → describing an unnecessary past effort.
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Must / Have to** → express _obligation_
    > You **must / have to** finish this today.

- **Should / Ought to** → express _mild advice or moral duty_
    > You **should** rest if you’re tired.
    
- **Need to** (positive form) → express _necessity_
    > You **need to** renew your passport.
    
- **Needn’t have + V3** → express _unnecessary past actions already done_
    > You **needn’t have bought** so many apples — I already had some.

# 12.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">Should, Ought to, Had Better</span>

### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
These three expressions are used to **give advice**, **make recommendations**, or **express mild obligation**, but they differ in tone and strength:

- **Should** → neutral advice or expectation.
    > “You should study harder.” (good idea, polite)
    
- **Ought to** → similar meaning to _should_, slightly more formal or moral.
    > “You ought to respect your parents.” (moral duty)
    
- **Had better** → strong advice or warning about possible bad results.
    > “You’d better leave now, or you’ll miss the bus.” (urgent or threat-like)
    

🧭 **Strength scale:**  
**Had better** > **Should ≈ Ought to**
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
| Type        | Structure                                             | Example                        | Meaning               |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Affirmative | should / ought to / had better + base verb            | You **should go** now.         | Advice                |
| Negative    | shouldn’t / ought not to / had better not + base verb | You **shouldn’t smoke** here.  | Warning / prohibition |
| Question    | Should / Ought I + base verb ...?                     | **Should I talk** to him?      | Asking advice         |
| Past advice | should / ought to + have + past participle            | You **should have called** me. | Regret about past     |
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
You **had better back up your cluster configuration**, otherwise you might lose critical data during an upgrade.
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
If you want to take part of our investigation, you had better prepare for that in advance, otherwise you won't be catching up. 
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _You had better to go now._ → ✅ _You had better go now._  
    🔹 Never use “to” after _had better_.
    
2. ❌ _You ought have told me._ → ✅ _You ought to have told me._  
    🔹 _Ought to_ always keeps **to**, even with _have_.
    
3. ⚠️ _Had better_ implies **warning**, not just advice.
    > “You’d better call her” = If you don’t, there’ll be trouble.  
    > “You should call her” = It’s a good idea.
    
4. ⚠️ Don’t confuse tense — _had better_ looks past but is **present** in meaning.
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **You should know better.** → expressing mild criticism.
2. **Ought to be careful.** → moral or polite advice.
3. **You’d better hurry up.** → urgent advice / warning.
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Must** → strong necessity or obligation.
    > You **must** wear a seatbelt.
    
- **Have to** → external rule or requirement.
    > You **have to** pass the exam to graduate.
    
- **Need to** → necessity (neutral).
    > You **need to** back up your files.
    
- **Could / Might want to** → very soft advice.
    > You **might want to** check the logs before redeploying.
    
- **Shall** (formal) → polite suggestion.
    > **Shall we** start the meeting?

# 13.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">Linking verbs: be, appear, seem, become, get, etc.</span>

### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
**Linking verbs** (also called _copular verbs_) connect the **subject** of a sentence with information that **describes or identifies it** — they do **not show action**.  
They link the subject to a **complement**, which can be a **noun**, **pronoun**, or **adjective** describing the subject.

> Subject + linking verb + complement

Examples:
- She **is** a doctor.
- It **seems** impossible.
- He **became** angry.
    
🧭 _The complement renames or describes the subject, not something else._
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
| Verb                                    | Function / Meaning                    | Example                                                           |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **be**                                  | expresses identity, state, or quality | He **is** tired.                                                  |
| **seem / appear**                       | express opinion or impression         | She **seems** nervous.                                            |
| **become / get / turn / go**            | show change of state                  | It **became** cold. / He **got** angry. / The sky **turned** red. |
| **feel / look / sound / smell / taste** | express perception through senses     | That **sounds** great. / The soup **tastes** awful.               |
| **remain / stay / keep**                | express continuation of a state       | He **remained** silent. / She **kept** calm.                      |

**Structure pattern:**

> Subject + Linking Verb + Complement (Adj / Noun / Noun Phrase)  
> e.g., “She became a teacher.” / “He looks tired.”
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
> Despite his calm tone, it **seemed** obvious that he was deeply frustrated by the delay.
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
Finally it turned out to be a unsolved challenge we didn't expect it became so hard-working.
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _He looks happily._ → ✅ _He looks happy._  
    🔹 Linking verbs take **adjectives**, not adverbs.
    > _He feels bad_, not _feels badly_.
    
2. ❌ _He became into a doctor._ → ✅ _He became a doctor._  
    🔹 Never use “into” after _become_.
    
3. ❌ _She got very tiredly after work._ → ✅ _She got very tired after work._
4. ⚠️ _Get_ and _become_ both show change, but _get_ is **less formal**.
    > _He became angry_ (formal), _He got angry_ (informal).
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **become aware / become interested / become clear**
2. **seem likely / seem impossible / seem happy**
3. **get tired / get dark / get lost**
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Action verbs vs linking verbs:**
    > _She smells the flowers_ (action) vs _She smells nice_ (linking).
    
- **State verbs:** _be, believe, know, love_ — describe conditions, not actions.
- **Change-of-state verbs:** _turn_, _grow_, _get_, _go_ (→ “The situation grew worse.”)
- **Causative contrast:**
    
    > _make + object + adj_ (He made me angry) ≠ _become + adj_ (I became angry).

# 14.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">GRAMMAR TOPIC</span>
Forming Passive Sentences 2: verb + -ing or to-infinitive
### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
Some verbs are followed by either **-ing** forms or **to-infinitives** in active sentences. When we turn these into **passive**, the structure changes depending on the verb and meaning:

- **Passive with -ing**:  
    Used after verbs that take a gerund (verb + -ing) when the subject of the sentence is not the "doer" of the action.
    > e.g., _She didn’t mind people criticising her_ → _She didn’t mind being criticised._
    
- **Passive with to-infinitive**:  
    Used with verbs that are typically followed by a **to-infinitive** in the active voice.
    > e.g., _They expect him to arrive early_ → _He is expected to arrive early._
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
|**Active Verb Pattern**|**Passive Equivalent**|
|---|---|
|Verb + object + to-infinitive|Subject + is/was + past participle + to-infinitive|
|Verb + -ing|Subject + verb + **being** + past participle|

#### 🔹 Example Structures
- _They asked us to wait_ → **We were asked to wait.**
- _She enjoys people helping her_ → **She enjoys being helped.**
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
> _The manager expects the team to complete the project ahead of schedule._  
> → _The team is expected to complete the project ahead of schedule._

> _I remember people always admiring her work._  
> → _I remember her work being admired._
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
My mood is being shaken by the thoughts that come up in my mind from time to time. It barely has no use trying to avoid them. It appears that they love being revised in my head.
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _They wanted that I go home early_ →  
    ✅ _They wanted me to go home early._
    
2. ❌ _She didn’t mind to be criticised._  
    ✅ _She didn’t mind **being criticised.**_
    
3. Mixing up **-ing** and **to-infinitive** when the verb clearly demands one:
    - _Enjoy_ always takes **-ing** → ✅ _She enjoys being praised._
    - _Expect_ takes **to-infinitive** → ✅ _He is expected to attend._
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **be expected to + verb**  
    → _You are expected to arrive on time._
2. **be heard/seen + -ing**  
    → _He was seen leaving the building._
3. **don’t mind + being + past participle**  
    → _I don’t mind being interrupted occasionally._
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Passive with causative verbs**:  
    _He made her cry_ → _She was made to cry_  
    (_Note_: "make" takes **bare infinitive** in active, but **to-infinitive** in passive)
    
- **Passive with reporting verbs**:  
    _People say he is a genius_ → _He is said to be a genius_
    
- **Passive of phrasal verbs**:  
    _They talked about her promotion_ → _Her promotion was talked about_

# 15.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">**Verb + -ing forms and infinitives (Part 1)**</span>

### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
Certain verbs in English are followed by either the **-ing form (gerund)** or the **to-infinitive**, and the choice can change the meaning. Knowing which structure to use depends on the verb.

- Some verbs are followed only by **-ing** (e.g., enjoy, avoid, suggest)
- Others are followed only by **to-infinitive** (e.g., decide, hope, plan)
- A few can be followed by **both**, sometimes with a **change in meaning** (e.g., remember, stop, try)
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>
|Verb Pattern|Example|
|---|---|
|Verb + **-ing**|I enjoy **reading** at night.|
|Verb + **to-infinitive**|She hopes **to pass** the exam.|
|Verb + object + to-infinitive|He asked **me to help** him.|
|Verb + -ing / to-infinitive (meaning changes)|_I stopped smoking._ vs _I stopped to smoke._|
### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
She remembered **locking the door** before she left.  
_(She has a memory of doing it.)_

She remembered **to lock the door** before she left.  
_(She didn’t forget to do it.)_
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
They wanted me to stop being rude, but tbh I wasn't intended making them shameful for me. 
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _I enjoy to swim._  
    ✅ _I enjoy swimming._
    
2. ❌ _He decided going home._  
    ✅ _He decided to go home._
    
3. Mixing verbs that change meaning:
    - _stop to do_ ≠ _stop doing_
    - _remember doing_ ≠ _remember to do_
        
4. Using object incorrectly:
    - ❌ _She made to cry me._
    - ✅ _She made me cry._
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **Avoid + -ing**  
    → _He avoids talking about politics._
    
2. **Promise + to-infinitive**  
    → _I promised to help him with his homework._
    
3. **Begin + -ing / to-infinitive (no change in meaning)**  
    → _She began to cry / She began crying._
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Bare infinitive**: _let me go, help her win_
- **Verb + object + infinitive**: _want him to leave    
- **Passive infinitives**: _She wants to be seen as confident_
- **Gerund as subject/object**: _Swimming is good for you_

# 16.10.2025
## <span style="color: LightBlue;">GRAMMAR TOPIC</span>
Verb + -ing forms and infinitives (2)
### <span style="color: Green;">Rule</span>
After some verbs, we can use different forms of the infinitive or -ing.  
The choice depends on meaning, time reference, or grammatical pattern.

Key points covered here:
1. **Negative forms** – _not to do_, _not doing_
2. **Perfect infinitive** – _to have + past participle_
3. **Perfect -ing** – _having + past participle_
4. *_Verb + preposition + object + to-infinitive_
5. *_Verb + object + to-infinitive_ (after certain verbs only)
### <span style="color: Yellow;">Forms & Structures</span>

|Structure|Form|Example|Notes|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Negative infinitive**|not + to + verb|We decided **not to go** to Paris.|The action is denied or refused.|
|**Negative -ing**|not + verb-ing|Some of my friends considered **not going** to college.|Used after verbs like _consider, avoid, suggest_.|
|**Perfect infinitive**|to have + past participle|The accident seems **to have happened** at 1 pm.|Used to show a **past action** after verbs like _seem, appear, allege, believe, say_.|
|**Perfect -ing**|having + past participle|I now regret **having bought** the car.|Used to talk about something done before another action or now regretted.|
|**Verb + for + object + to-infinitive**|for + someone + to + verb|They arranged **for Rania to stay** in London.|You must include _for_ before the object.|
|**Verb + at / on / to + object + to-infinitive**|preposition + object + to + verb|I shouted **at the man to open** the door. / We’re depending **on you to find** a solution. / He signalled **to the pilot to take off.**|The preposition depends on the main verb.|
|**Verb + object + to-infinitive**|someone + to + verb|The police warned **everyone to stay** inside.|Many verbs require an object before _to_.|

### <span style="color: Orange;">1 High-level Example</span>
The driver **appeared to have forgotten** the route, but everyone **pretended not to notice.**
### <span style="color: Grey;">My Personal Example</span>
It was considered not following that way, everyone got scared of the danger of that decision. 
### <span style="color: Blue;">Common Mistakes & Traps🌓</span>
1. ❌ _They arranged Rania to stay in London._  
    ✅ _They arranged **for Rania to stay** in London._  
    (You must include _for_ before the object.)
    
2. ❌ _The police warned to stay inside._  
    ✅ _The police warned **everyone to stay** inside._  
    (An object is required before _to_.)
    
3. ❌ _I regret to have bought the car._  
    ✅ _I regret **having bought** the car._  
    (Use **having + past participle** after _regret_ when referring to the past.)
    
4. Confusing verb-preposition patterns:
    - **shout at** someone to do something
    - **depend on** someone to do something
    - **signal to** someone to do something
### <span style="color: Purple;">3 Famous Uses / Collocations</span>
1. **Seem / appear + to have + past participle**  
    → _He seems to have forgotten my name._
    
2. **Regret / remember / deny + having + past participle**  
    → _She denied having stolen the money._
    
3. **Arrange / apply / wait + for + object + to + verb**  
    → _They applied for the meeting to be postponed._
### <span style="color: Red;">Other Related Grammar Forms</span>
- **Verb + object + bare infinitive** → _make, let, help_  
    (e.g. _She made me cry._ / _He let me go._)
    
- **Verb + to-infinitive (without object)** → _decide, hope, plan_  
    (e.g. _We decided to stay._)
    
- **Verb + -ing** → _enjoy, consider, avoid, deny_  
    (e.g. _She enjoys swimming._)
    
- **Passive with perfect infinitive** → _He is said to have worked there._
